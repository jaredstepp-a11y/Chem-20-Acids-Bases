The Arrhenius theory of acids and bases

Svante Arrhenius defined acids and bases by what they release when dissolved in water: an acid produces hydrogen ions (H⁺) and a base produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in aqueous solution.

Operational vs. theoretical definitions▼

Chemists describe acids and bases in two ways:

Operational definitions describe what we observe in the lab — the properties used to identify a substance:

• Acids: taste sour, turn blue litmus red, react with active metals to release H₂ gas, and conduct electricity.

• Bases: taste bitter, feel slippery, turn red litmus blue, and conduct electricity.

Theoretical definitions explain why — Arrhenius theory says these properties come from the ions produced in water.

Operational = what you see and measure. Theoretical = the explanation for those observations. Both are important in Chemistry 20.
What acids and bases produce in water▼

An Arrhenius acid ionizes in water to release H⁺ (which immediately bonds to water to form the hydronium ion, H₃O⁺):

HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)

An Arrhenius base dissociates in water to release OH⁻:

NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
Neutralization happens when an acid and base react: the H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form water (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O), leaving a salt behind.
Limitations of the Arrhenius theory▼

The Arrhenius theory works well but has limits:

• It only applies to aqueous (water) solutions.

• It can't explain bases that don't contain OH⁻ — for example, ammonia (NH₃) is clearly basic, but has no hydroxide to release directly.

These gaps are solved by the Brønsted–Lowry theory (acids donate protons, bases accept them), which you'll meet in Chemistry 30. For Chemistry 20, the Arrhenius model is your main tool.

Strong vs. weak acids and bases

The strength of an acid or base describes how completely it ionizes in water — not how much is dissolved. Strong acids and bases ionize almost completely; weak ones ionize only partially.

Degree of ionization▼

Strong acids and bases ionize essentially 100% — nearly every molecule breaks apart into ions.

Weak acids and bases ionize only partially — most of the molecules stay intact, and only a small fraction form ions.

Common strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄. Common strong bases: Group 1 hydroxides like NaOH and KOH.

Acetic acid (CH₃COOH, in vinegar) is a classic weak acid — at any moment only about 1% of its molecules are ionized.
Strength vs. concentration — don't mix them up!▼

This is the most commonly confused idea in the whole unit. Strength and concentration are independent properties:

• Concentration = how much acid is dissolved per litre (dilute vs. concentrated).

• Strength = what fraction of it ionizes (weak vs. strong).

You can have a dilute strong acid (e.g. 0.001 mol/L HCl) or a concentrated weak acid (e.g. 10 mol/L acetic acid). "Strong" does not mean "concentrated," and "weak" does not mean "dilute."
Conductivity and ionization▼

Electric current in a solution is carried by ions, so the more ions present, the better the solution conducts.

At the same concentration, a strong acid conducts strongly (lots of ions) while a weak acid conducts weakly (few ions). This is one operational way to tell them apart in the lab.

A bright bulb in a conductivity tester signals many ions (strong); a dim bulb signals few ions (weak). A non-conductor would have essentially no ions at all.

pH & pOH calculations

pH is a number from 0 to 14 that measures how acidic or basic a solution is, based on its hydrogen ion concentration. pOH does the same using hydroxide ions.

The pH scale▼

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14 at 25°C:

• pH < 7 → acidic   • pH = 7 → neutral   • pH > 7 → basic

pH = −log[H⁺]     [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ
The scale is logarithmic — each whole pH unit is a 10× change in [H⁺]. A pH 2 solution has 10 times the [H⁺] of pH 3, and 100 times that of pH 4.
pOH and the water relationship▼

pOH measures hydroxide concentration the same way pH measures hydrogen ions:

pOH = −log[OH⁻]     [OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ

In any aqueous solution at 25°C, pH and pOH always add to 14:

pH + pOH = 14
This comes from the self-ionization of water: [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴. If you know one of pH or pOH, you can always find the other.
Worked example: both directions▼

Finding pH from concentration: A solution has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.

pH = −log(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 4.00  (acidic)

Finding concentration from pH: A solution has pH = 9.0.

[H⁺] = 10⁻⁹ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁹ mol/L pOH = 14 − 9 = 5, so [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁵ mol/L
Tip: when [H⁺] is written as 1.0 × 10⁻ⁿ, the pH is simply n. The math gets more involved only when the coefficient isn't exactly 1.0.

Acid-base indicators

An indicator is a substance that changes colour depending on the pH of a solution. Each indicator switches colour over a specific, narrow pH range.

How indicators work▼

Most indicators are themselves weak acids whose acid form is one colour and whose ionized (conjugate base) form is a different colour.

As the pH changes, the balance between these two forms shifts, and the colour changes. Each indicator flips over its own characteristic pH range.

Because the change happens over a range (not a single pH), a single indicator tells you roughly where the pH is — not an exact value.
Common indicators and their ranges▼

Four indicators you should know for Chemistry 20:

• Methyl orange: red (below 3.1) → yellow (above 4.4)

• Litmus: red (below ~5) → blue (above ~8)

• Bromothymol blue: yellow (below 6.0) → blue (above 7.6)

• Phenolphthalein: colourless (below 8.3) → pink (above 10.0)

Phenolphthalein is the most common titration indicator — it's easy to spot the moment a colourless solution turns faint pink.
Estimating pH with indicators▼

One indicator narrows the pH to a range. To estimate more precisely, you can use several indicators together — or a universal indicator, which is a blend that shows a continuous rainbow of colours across the whole pH scale.

For an exact pH value, a pH meter (electronic probe) is used instead of indicators. Indicators are quick and visual; meters are precise.

Titration analysis

A titration is a technique for finding the unknown concentration of an acid or base by reacting it with a solution of known concentration (the titrant) until they exactly neutralize.

How a titration works▼

A known-concentration titrant is added from a burette to a measured volume of the unknown solution, drop by drop, until the reaction is exactly complete.

• Equivalence point: the moles of acid and base are stoichiometrically equal (the true endpoint of the reaction).

• Endpoint: the point where the indicator changes colour — chosen to match the equivalence point as closely as possible.

Good technique means doing several trials and averaging the volumes that agree closely (concordant results) for an accurate answer.
The neutralization calculation▼

At the equivalence point, use the mole ratio from the balanced equation. For a 1:1 reaction (like HCl + NaOH), the moles of acid equal the moles of base:

moles = concentration × volume  (n = C × V) Cₐ × Vₐ = C_b × V_b  (for a 1:1 ratio)

For other ratios, scale by the coefficients. Example: H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH means moles of NaOH = 2 × moles of H₂SO₄.

Always start from the balanced equation to get the correct mole ratio — it's the most common place to lose marks.
Worked example▼

25.0 mL of NaOH is neutralized by 18.5 mL of 0.100 mol/L HCl. Find [NaOH]. (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, a 1:1 ratio.)

moles HCl = 0.100 × 0.0185 = 1.85 × 10⁻³ mol moles NaOH = 1.85 × 10⁻³ mol (1:1 ratio) [NaOH] = 1.85 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0740 mol/L
Watch your units: convert millilitres to litres (÷ 1000) before using n = C × V.

Titration curves & choosing an indicator

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added. Its shape depends on the acid and base used — and tells you which indicator will work best.

The shapes of titration curves▼

The pH at the equivalence point depends on the strengths of the acid and base:

• Strong acid + strong base → equivalence at pH 7 (neutral)

• Weak acid + strong base → equivalence at pH > 7 (basic)

• Strong acid + weak base → equivalence at pH < 7 (acidic)

Every curve has a steep, near-vertical jump near the equivalence point — that's where a single drop of titrant causes a big pH change.
Choosing the right indicator▼

The golden rule: the indicator's colour-change range should fall within the steep jump of the curve, bracketing the equivalence point pH.

• Strong–strong (pH 7) → bromothymol blue (6.0–7.6)

• Weak acid–strong base (pH > 7) → phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0)

• Strong acid–weak base (pH < 7) → methyl orange (3.1–4.4)

If you pick an indicator whose range misses the steep part, it will change colour at the wrong volume and give you an inaccurate result.
Endpoint vs. equivalence point▼

These two terms are often mixed up:

• The equivalence point is the true point where acid and base are stoichiometrically equal.

• The endpoint is what you actually observe — the moment the indicator changes colour.

A well-chosen indicator makes the endpoint and equivalence point occur at almost the same volume, so your observed result matches the real one.
